The idea in one paragraph
Another company supplies the games and tells us what each player scored. ChartVolt does everything else — exactly as it does for trading today.
A player buys credits, pays an entry fee to join a competition or to challenge someone, plays, and the winners share the pot. Points, ranking, levels, badges and milestones all follow. The only thing that changes is who supplies the gameplay in the middle.
1

Why this is the low-risk way to add games

Building games in-house means designing, building and maintaining each one. Buying them means we get a catalogue on day one.

Speed

A provider brings a full catalogue immediately. We add titles by ticking a box, not by writing a game.

Focus

Our advantage is the competition engine, the money handling and the rewards — not building puzzle games. This plays to it.

Variety

Different players like different games. A catalogue widens the audience far faster than one in-house title ever could.

The concept does not change

Buy credits → pay an entry fee → compete → winners share the pot. That is what ChartVolt does today with trading, and it is exactly what it will do with an external game. The engine that handles the money, the ranking and the rewards is already built, already live and already reconciled. This project does not touch it.

2

The single most important design decision

The provider never touches money. They run games and report scores — that is the whole relationship.

What this boundary buys us

Because the provider never touches money…
There is no wallet interface to host for them, and no provider-initiated debits or credits
No third party can move a customer balance, under any circumstances
Our financial reporting, reconciliation and audit trail are completely untouched
A provider outage can delay a contest, but it cannot corrupt money
Switching provider, or running several at once, has no effect on the money layer
The regulatory position is unchanged: entry fee in, ranked by skill, fixed prize by finishing position
A hard rule

Any provider who requires access to player balances, or who wants to take bets and pay winnings directly, is rejected on that basis alone. It is not a negotiation point. It is the difference between a contained integration and rebuilding our financial controls around a third party.

4

Two kinds of game, and why it decides everything

This distinction determines which games can be used for competitions and which can only be used for challenges. It has nothing to do with how many people are competing.

Read this before the diagram: “independent play” does not mean “playing alone”

Independent play describes the gameplay, not the contest. It means each player plays their own round and gets their own score, rather than needing a live opponent in the same session. Every one of those players is still ranked against every other player, and they are all still competing for the same pot.

Trading is itself an independent-play game. Every trader in a ChartVolt competition trades their own account, alone, at their own pace — and they are all ranked against each other for a share of one prize pool. Nobody would call a trading competition a solo activity, and the same reasoning applies to a trivia or chess-puzzle competition.

There is no single-player paid format anywhere in this plan, and none is proposed.

The minimum-players rule is reused, not built. Competition.minParticipants is already on the model, and competition.actions.ts already auto-cancels and refunds every entry fee when a competition does not fill. A provider-game competition inherits all of it, because it is the same model and the same code path.

Correction, 7 September 2026 — inheriting the code path is not inheriting the behaviour (R43)

The paragraph above was right about the design and wrong about the platform as it actually ran, and the distinction is the whole lesson. The code path was indeed shared. It also did not refund anyone.

The every-minute sweep that cancels an unfilled competition set status: "cancelled" itself and then called the refund. The refund claims its competition with status: { $ne: "cancelled" } — the lock that stops a double refund — so the claim matched nothing, the refund returned success having moved no money, and every player's entry fee stayed with the platform against a competition that displayed as cancelled. It affected trading and provider contests identically, because, exactly as this paragraph says, it is the same code path.

Fixed 7 September 2026, at the caller and again in the refund itself, with idempotency moved off the status and onto the per-player ledger rows. The fix is not retroactive. Affected contests can be identified — cancelled, participants not marked refunded, no refund rows — but whether to compensate those players is an owner decision that is still open.

Why this correction is left in place rather than the paragraph simply being reworded: "it is the same model and the same code path" is a genuinely good argument, it was the right design, and it will be made again about the next capability. It is just not evidence that the capability works. See R43 in the risk register.

The practical answer for chess

If chess is wanted at launch, use chess puzzles rather than full chess. Puzzles are independent-play, so they rank cleanly in a competition and need no bracket engine. It is recognisably chess, and it works with what exists. Full head-to-head chess can follow later as challenges.

What we require from a provider

Three calls out, one call in. The narrowness is deliberate — it is what makes a second provider cheap to add and any provider cheap to drop.

DirCallWhat it does
outGET /gamesList the games, with what each one can and cannot do
outPOST /roundsStart a round for one player, and get back a link to play it
outGET /rounds/{id}Ask what happened, if the result never arrived
inPOST /eventsThe provider tells us the score, signed, server to server

The twelve things that are not negotiable

A provider failing any one of these cannot be used for contests where real money is at stake.

Results arrive server to server, never through the player's browser
Results are cryptographically signed, with a timestamp so old ones can be rejected
We can ask for a result at any time, in case the message to us is lost
Repeated messages are safely ignored — one round can only ever produce one score
Every round always finishes in a known state. A round can never simply hang
All players in one competition can be given identical content from a seed we supply
There is a test environment, separate from the live one
They handle no money of any kind
Each game states whether a higher or lower score wins
Game identifiers are stable and never reused
There is a written service level agreement
Each round has a replay or audit link, so disputes can be answered
One detail that pays for itself

Each game must describe its own settings in a machine-readable form. If it does, the admin panel can build the settings form automatically, which means a new title from the provider goes live by ticking a box rather than by writing and deploying code. This is the difference between an integration that scales and one that needs a developer for every new game.

7

How it plugs in

The contest engine never learns that a provider exists. It only knows that players have scores.

The rule that protects the whole design

Provider concepts must never leak past the adapter. If the provider's identifiers, formats or vocabulary start appearing in the prize logic, the leaderboards or the admin reports, then switching or adding a provider stops being a swap and becomes a rewrite. The test is simple: adding a second provider should touch one folder and one registry entry, and nothing else.

Plugging out again — the half nobody asks about

Games have to be plug and play in both directions. A new game must appear in every statistic, ranking and league table with no extra code — and turning a game off must break nothing. The first half is designed above. The second half is where the real danger sits, because it is the one that can damage figures a player has already earned.

Automatically included means automatically excluded

If a player's overall points are worked out by adding up the games that are currently switched on, then switching a game off quietly takes away everything earned in it. A player who reached level 12 partly through a provider game drops to level 9 the moment an operator disables that game for a commercial reason. Their rank falls, badges may stop qualifying, and nothing reports a problem — the pages still load, the numbers are simply smaller. The player experiences it as their progress being taken away, and support has no way to explain it.

The uncomfortable part is that adding up the enabled games is the obvious way to build this. It reads correctly, it survives review, and it is only wrong on the day somebody changes a setting — months after the code was written and far from whoever wrote it. That is why the decision is taken in the foundation phase rather than left to the phase where the symptom would appear.

Three rules, free if adopted now, expensive later
  • Points, levels and badges are banked, not recalculated. Once earned, they are a permanent record that only ever moves forward — never a sum re-derived from today's list of games.
  • Disabling a game hides it and stops new entries. It never touches history. The game page disappears, no new contests can be created, matchmaking stops suggesting it — and not one earned record changes.
  • A withdrawn game's history is retired, not deleted. Its league table is hidden or marked retired and the records stay, so a player can always see and explain their own past.

Contests already under way finish normally, or are cancelled with full refunds — the same behaviour the platform already uses when trading is switched off. A paid entry is never left stranded. Getting this wrong is not a code fix later; it is a migration across every player's progression, which is exactly the kind of work this programme exists to avoid.

Where a provider game appears to players

Since this plan was drafted, the platform direction has firmed up: players will browse a catalogue of games, each with its own page, rather than a single list of competitions. That has a direct and useful consequence for provider games.

A provider title becomes a catalogue entry, and nothing more

Each game pulled from the provider's catalogue is published as a catalogue entry — the same kind of record that an in-house game uses. It gets its own page with a description, rules, artwork and its own league table, and it can be featured, reordered, hidden or marked coming-soon from the admin panel without a release. To a player, a provider game and an in-house game are indistinguishable, which is exactly the intended outcome. The provider is a supplier, not a brand on our site.

What this asks of the provider

A game page needs more than a name. The provider must supply page-quality content: a real description, a short rules summary, how-to-play text, and artwork at usable sizes — not just a thumbnail. This is cheap for them to provide and expensive for us to write on their behalf for every title, so it belongs in the contract from the start. It is listed in the requirements document sent to providers.

One commercial limit that follows automatically

The platform rule is that nothing sold may improve a score or ranking in a paid contest. So no provider mechanic that sells advantage — extra time, hints, retries, continues, or paid unlocks that affect results — may be enabled inside a ranked contest, whoever takes the payment. If a provider's game depends on such mechanics for its economics, it is the wrong game for this platform. Ask early.

Fairness — everyone faces the same challenge

Two players cannot be ranked against each other if they answered different questions of unknown difficulty.

The exploit this closes, and the one it creates

Seeding makes the contest fair, but it also means a player who sees the content early has an enormous advantage. So an attempt is used up the moment the round is created, not when it is finished. Without that, a player could open a round, read the questions, quit, look up the answers, and start again. Consuming the attempt at the start removes the value of doing so entirely.

Trusting a number that decides real money

A score arrives over the internet from a third party and determines who gets paid. Every one goes through the same gate.

What we accept from the browser

  • "The game finished, refresh the screen"
  • "Close the window"
  • "Resize to this height"

What we never accept from the browser

  • "My score was 9,800"
  • "I completed the round"
  • Anything that affects money

A lesson from a defect found in our own code

On 1 September 2026, while re-reading the contest entry code for the prerequisite fixes, we found a set of internal testing endpoints that could add credits to any wallet — and the only thing protecting them was a label the caller attached to their own request. No secret, no password. It was fixed and deployed the same day, and it is mentioned here rather than buried because the same mistakes are available in the callback described above, where the stake is a score that decides prize money. A fourth was added on 2 September 2026, from a second endpoint of the same shape found in the player app.

A lock that opens when unconfigured

The check accepted the request whenever the secret had not been set. Missing configuration became permission. A security check must refuse when it does not know what to compare against — never wave the request through.

A lock never fitted to the door

A correct check already existed. Nineteen of twenty endpoints simply did not call it. Test the endpoint, not the checking function — a passing test on the helper proves nothing about the routes.

A convenient bypass left in

A shortcut added to make local development easy was still active in production. Any development bypass must be tied to the environment and proved inactive outside it.

A label instead of a lock

Found 2 September 2026, and the third of this shape. A page in the player app was marked "admin only" on all three of its functions and checked only that somebody was logged in — letting any player raise a rival's fraud score, or wipe their own. Nothing called it, which is exactly why nobody looked. A comment is not a check, and an endpoint nothing calls is still reachable. Delete dead endpoints rather than leaving them to be assumed safe.

All four apply directly to the signature verification on the result callback. They are written into the acceptance criteria for the phase that builds it, and recorded as R27 in the risk register.

Why points come from position and not from score

A trivia score of 8,730 and a chess-puzzle time of 42 seconds cannot be compared. But first place out of forty means the same thing in any game. Because points are calculated from finishing position, field size and stake, a brand new game from the provider works with the points system on day one, with no changes at all.

Badges come in three scopes

Platform-wide

First deposit, verified account, ten contests entered, first win. Available to provider-game players immediately, at no content cost.

Per game

Score over 1,000 in Trivia Blitz, a chess puzzle solved in under thirty seconds. Configured in admin, not written in code.

Across games

Won a contest in three different games. A genuinely new category that rewards breadth and only becomes possible now.

Two rules that protect player progress

Never show a player badges for games they do not play — a list of unreachable achievements discourages rather than motivates. And never rename a badge's internal identifier: those identifiers are the keys to saved progress, and renaming one silently deletes what players have earned. The visible label can change freely at any time.

Ask this one first

"Is the outcome determined by the player's decisions, or by a random number generator?" Everything else follows from the answer. A provider can describe chance-based products as "games" throughout their marketing. If the catalogue mentions return-to-player percentages, house edge, volatility or free spins, the answer is chance — and the regulatory position of the entire platform changes.

The commercial question that decides viability

Provider pricing determines whether cheap contests can exist at all, and it is easy to discover this too late. Take a €1 entry fee, twenty players, and a 10% platform fee — a €20 pool and €2 of revenue.

Price per roundOne attempt eachBest of threeOutcome
€0.01€0.20€0.60Fine
€0.05€1.00€3.00Best of three loses money
€0.10€2.00€6.00Break-even at best
Two conclusions to carry into the negotiation

Pricing per round above roughly two cents makes low-entry contests unviable — and those are exactly the contests that attract new players. And any per-round pricing must come with a cap on attempts, because unlimited replays at a cost per replay is an open-ended liability. A flat licence or a share of our fee is far preferable to paying per round.

Built 4 September 2026 — a competition on a provider game can now be published, joined and correctly ordered. Publishing re-runs the whole safety checklist against the saved competition rather than trusting the checks done on the day it was drafted, because a draft can sit for a week and in that week the game can be switched off. Ordering asks the game which way is better and sorts accordingly, so a puzzle scored upward and a time trial scored downward both rank correctly through one mechanism, with no game-specific code in the engine. Two faults were found and fixed on the way, and both were the quiet kind: a player joining a game competition could not be recorded at all, because the record demanded three pieces of trading information that a chess player does not have; and any player who was recorded got filed under trading, permanently, because the game name was being left to a default. Neither would have produced an error message anyone could act on. Then, later the same day, the rest of it: a player can play a round and the winners are paid. The way this was built matters more than the fact of it. Paying out a game competition needed the parts of the existing payout process that are about money — working out the prizes, taking the platform fee, paying the game companies' referrers, crediting the winners, recording the final table — without the parts that are about trading, which close open positions and price them. So those money steps were lifted out of the trading payout code and made shareable, and trading was then pointed at the shared copies. The alternative, writing a second payout routine for games, is the single change in this whole project most capable of paying the wrong people. The proof that nothing moved is that the existing payout and ranking tests still produce identical figures, and that proof only holds because nothing else was altered at the same time. Three faults in the existing trading payout came to light as a result, which is the strongest argument for having done it this way: the stored final table had never actually saved whether a place was tied or whether a player qualified, on any competition ever settled; a tie between two wiped-out accounts produced nonsense prize figures; and every failed payout had been recorded in the logs as the bare words “Failed to finalize” with the reason thrown away. Finished later the same day, and it closed two gaps rather than the one being tracked. A competition can be set up so that a player whose result never arrives is removed from the ranking and given their entry fee back. That refund is now paid automatically, and the prize pool is reduced by it in the same single operation, so the pot can never be paid out while it still counts a player who has left. The gap nobody had written down was the neighbouring one: a competition could also be set up to hold settlement until a person looks at it, and that setting had never actually stopped anything — the competition settled on time and paid out exactly as though the setting were absent. It was missed for a simple reason worth remembering: the refund was recorded as outstanding in four separate documents, and the hold was recorded in none, so the one that was written down four times was the only one anybody chased. Two further faults surfaced underneath, both worse than the gap being closed. Removing a player had never actually removed them from the ranking, only labelled them, so a player could have been paid a prize and given their entry fee back at the same time. And a settlement that correctly refused to run would have left the competition in a state no later attempt could pick up — permanently unsettled, with nobody paid, and no error raised. What it still does not do: the whole sequence is reachable by the system and by tests, not yet by clicking — the buttons are in the admin and player phases. Two serious faults were found on 5 September 2026, a day after this was called finished, and both would have paid the wrong players. The first: the score a player earned was recorded against the round they played but never carried up to their entry in the competition, which is the figure the ranking actually reads. Every player would therefore have finished on nought, tied in first place, and taken an equal share of the prize pool no matter how well they played. The second: the question of which direction a game scores in — higher is better for a puzzle, lower is better for a race — was being read from a place where the answer was never stored, so the answer was always “higher is better”. Every race or time trial would have ranked upside down and paid the slowest player first. Neither would have produced an error. Both are fixed, and no competition had ever been settled, so no player was affected and there is nothing to correct after the fact. The reason they survived is the part worth keeping: the payout code carried a comment stating plainly that the score was already being carried up, and it was not; and the tests that check the ranking supply the scores themselves, so they proved that ranking works when given scores and could never notice that no score ever arrived. A third fault of the same family was found on 7 September 2026, and it was larger than either: the admin panel’s own copy of the payout process refused to settle game competitions altogether. The platform has two programs that finish competitions — the player-facing site and the admin panel — and both check every minute for competitions that have ended. The player-facing one knew how to settle a game competition. The admin panel’s did not: it only knew how to settle a trading one, so when a game competition came up it declined it and left it marked as still running. Which of the two happened to look first therefore decided whether a competition was ever paid out at all — and when the admin panel won that race, nobody was paid, nothing was ranked, and the competition simply sat there past its end time looking finished to every screen and to the operator. No error was raised on either outcome. It is fixed by giving the admin panel the same six lines the player-facing site has had since early September, and no competition had ever been settled this way, so again nobody was affected and there is nothing to correct after the fact. Two things about how it hid are worth keeping, because both are checks we rely on. The specialist code for settling game competitions had already been copied into the admin panel weeks earlier — it was simply never called — so the automated check that the two programs hold matching copies of everything reported no problem, entirely correctly: the copies did match, and only the instruction to use them was missing. And the clue that exposed the previous fault in this same pair of files — one copy being far larger than the other — is now useless here, because the missing piece is six lines long. The general lesson, which now replaces the two individual cases: these two programs each hold their own copy of the process that finishes a competition, and teaching one of them something new does not teach the other. Every capability has to be checked in both. The owner's question — what happens when one player has finished and another is still going — was answered on 7 September 2026, and answering it exposed a fault that would have taken money off a player who had done nothing wrong. Half the answer was already right: every player gets the same window, no round can outlive the competition it belongs to, and a player who starts too late to finish is told so rather than being cut off part-way. What was wrong was the moment of handover. The competition was being scored and paid out within about a minute of its own closing time — before the short waiting period that exists precisely because an outside game company does not report a score instantly. So a player who finished at 13:59:50 had their score arrive a few seconds later, find the competition already settled, and be filed as a late result and thrown away: they were ranked on nothing and paid nothing for a round they had genuinely completed. From that player's seat it is indistinguishable from being cheated, and the only trace it left was an internal audit note nobody is watching. Settling now waits out that period before doing anything, and it refuses an operator who tries to force it early — telling them the time it can run instead — because forcing it two minutes after the close would destroy the scores of everyone who finished in the last minute, and they would never know they had done it. A second, larger gap sat underneath that one. An operator setting up a competition chooses what should happen to a round that never reports: score it zero and pay everyone else on time, remove that player and refund their entry fee, or hold the whole competition for a human to look at. Two of those three had never been capable of happening. The recovery process that was supposed to mark such a round as needing a decision is written but not yet scheduled to run — it belongs to a later phase — so nothing in the running system ever marked one, the operator's choice had nothing to act on, and a round nobody finished simply stayed open for ever against a competition that had ended weeks earlier. Closing that off is now part of settling, so all three choices work for the first time. The detail that matters most in it is a single word: those rounds are recorded as unresolved rather than void. Void is the tidier-looking word and it would have been silently wrong — it means “this attempt produced nothing and nobody is owed anything”, which would have overridden the operator's choice on every competition, including the ones set to refund the player. Those two questions — what a competition pays when nobody scored, and where the share of a prize position nobody finished in goes — were answered the same day, and the answer was that a player who never played was being paid. Every test of whether an entrant deserves a prize had been written for trading: was the account wiped out, did they place enough trades, was their win rate high enough. A game competition switches all three off, correctly, because a puzzle has no trades and no account to wipe out — so nothing was left to disqualify anybody at all. Somebody who never started a single round was treated as having scored zero, and zero is a score. Worked through with the owner's own example of ten entrants and prizes of 70%, 20% and 10%: one player actually played, and the two who never started took 30% of the pot between them. Worse, if nobody played, all ten tied for first and split the entire prize pool — the exact opposite of the rule that if there is no winner, nobody wins.

The part worth knowing is that this looked like a completely different fault. On screen it appears as a prize split that makes no sense, which is how it was reported, and the code that divides the pot was right the whole time — it already hands an unclaimed position's share upward to the players who did finish, which is exactly what was asked for. Reading that code would have found nothing, however carefully and however many times. What was missing sat one step earlier, in deciding who was on the list in the first place. The fix asks the game itself: a game competition requires a real score, a trading competition does not, because a flat trading account is a genuine result and the operator already has a minimum-trades setting to say otherwise. That last question — what a competition pays when nobody at all scores — was put to the owner and answered, and the answer was neither of the two options as put. It is now a choice the operator makes per competition, in the setup wizard: refund the entrants, or send the pot where a trading competition's unclaimed prizes go. Two defaults deliberately differ and will be read as a mistake if they are not explained: new competitions default to refunding, because a competition that produced no result is most likely an outage; every existing competition keeps the old behaviour, because a stored setting cannot be changed retroactively without changing the terms people already entered under. The refund is net of the platform fee, unlike a cancellation, because the competition genuinely ran. Three neighbouring rules are not configurable and must not be confused with it: a competition cancelled for want of players refunds in full with no fee, an outright failure does the same, and a player disqualified for breaking a rule keeps nothing.

The last item from the owner's report was answered on 7 September 2026, and it too turned out to be a fault rather than a preference. And the fix described above — the one that stops a player who never played from being paid — did not work on the day it was made. That was found and closed later on 7 September 2026, and it is the most instructive thing in this section. How a Game Master actually earns — not what the name suggests

This is the single fact most likely to be assumed wrongly, and a plan built on the opposite assumption would be wrong throughout.

The mechanism

A Game Master earns from the entry fees of players they referred, in any contest those players enter — not from contests the Game Master created. Creating competitions is how they attract and keep referred players. It is not itself the revenue event.

The consequence is convenient: a Game Master earns correctly from a provider contest the moment such a contest can be settled at all, with no Game Master-specific work. The share is a percentage of the entry fee, capped so it can never exceed the platform fee, and neither of those calculations knows what a game is.

The problem trading never had

A trading round costs nothing to run. A provider round has a price. The Game Master's share is a percentage of the entry fee, calculated at settlement before that cost exists, and the safety cap is against the gross platform fee — so the cap does not save us.

The decision
Game Masters earn from provider contests from day one. They cannot create one until the share is calculated on net fee.
The permission list defaults to trading only, so no existing Game Master silently gains the ability to create a loss-making contest. Because earning follows referred players rather than created contests, the revenue share works from the first provider competition an admin runs. Only creation is held back — until the share is computed on net platform fee after provider cost, and a minimum entry fee is in place. That split costs nothing and blocks nothing permanently. The list itself was built on 7 September 2026 and defaults exactly as described, so the gate is now enforced rather than planned; what remains held back is the same thing, for the same commercial reason.

The finding, and it survives the decision
Buying the games instead of building one is not the cheaper route. It is the broader one.
Dropping the in-house game saves about three and a half weeks. Integrating a provider properly — signed results, duplicates, rounds that never report, reconciliation — costs about seven. And every other piece of work is identical, because it is platform work that has nothing to do with who supplies the gameplay. Four risks that only exist on this route

We cannot prove it without them

With a game we built, the architecture is validated by our own code. Here, everything past the fifth week is blocked on someone else's test environment. A provider slow to grant access stops the project dead, with nothing to show. Get a committed date, not a promise.

One supplier, no fallback

If they terminate, raise prices or fail commercially, the platform reverts to trading alone and every week of games work is stranded. There is no in-house game to fall back on. Evaluate a second provider before launch even if only one goes live. This is the risk the 2 September decision made real rather than hypothetical — it is the same risk as before, with its escape route closed.

No cost floor

A game we built costs nothing per contest, so free entry contests are always viable for attracting players. A fee per round can make exactly those contests loss-making. Settle pricing before the integration, not after.

A Game Master contest that loses money

The sharpest version of the cost problem, and the easiest to miss because every part behaves correctly on its own. The Game Master's share is a slice of the entry fee taken before the provider is paid. Gate creation until the share is calculated on the fee that is actually left. Section 15

The hedge worth considering — now with a deadline

Build the foundation, integrate the provider — and keep one small in-house game on the backlog as insurance. Not as the launch game, but as a two-to-three week project that can be pulled forward if the provider relationship goes wrong. It turns the single-supplier risk from existential into merely inconvenient, and it gives the architecture the second real implementation that proves the whole design works.

The decision on 2 September gave this a date. The answer is needed before we start building against a specific provider's test environment, because that is the point at which the programme becomes committed to one supplier. After it, the hedge is still possible but no longer cheap.

The game became playable by a person on 6 September 2026, and that too found a fault nothing else could have. Both puzzles now run in a browser and on a phone: you drag a finger between matching numbers, fill the grid, submit, and the score is worked out by the service and reported back over the signed connection. The score is never sent by the browser and there is deliberately no field for one, because the player has a developer console. What building a screen exposed was that a round nobody had started was reporting itself as finished — so the first thing a paying player would have seen is the results page for a game they had not played. It had been invisible for as long as there was no screen, because every other caller in the system asks that question only after the game has begun. What is still missing is the connection between the two halves: the game company has never been registered through the admin screens and no round has travelled between them.

The game was made ready to put on a server later the same day, and writing the instructions for doing so found three more faults. Everything a deployment needs now exists — the process definition, an annotated template for its settings, and a step-by-step guide covering the four passwords and the admin clicks that switch it on. Nothing has been deployed. The pattern here is worth naming, because it is the same one that found the login fault: writing down exactly what somebody has to do is a test of whether they can actually do it. Three of those written instructions turned out to be untrue. The admin panel refused to register the game at all, because it demanded a secure web address and the game sits on the same machine as the platform, where the local connection is the safest one available rather than a relaxed one. And two settings had defaults that were harmless in testing and dangerous in production, both failing in the way that is hardest to catch: had either been forgotten, the service would have started normally, the catalogue would have synchronised, a competition could have been published — and every player's game window would have pointed at their own computer, or the game would have been embeddable by any website on the internet. No error, no log entry, nothing for anyone to notice. All three are fixed, and the service now refuses to start in production if either setting is missing.

The route the game reaches players by was then changed on the owner's instruction, and the reason is worth recording: the original plan asked for changes to the live server that the owner was not willing to make. Putting the game on its own web address needs a new domain entry, a new block of web-server configuration and a new security certificate — three operations on a machine already serving customers. The game is now served through the main site instead, at an address the site was not already using. Deploying it becomes the same routine update as any other change: fetch the code, rebuild, start the process. Nothing that could take the existing sites down is involved.

The trade is real and is recorded rather than glossed. The game window is now on the same web address as the platform, so one of the browser-level protections — the check that the game is speaking from the address we expect — is satisfied automatically rather than genuinely tested. Everything about the supplier connection itself is unaffected, because none of it involves a browser: the signed requests, the round bookkeeping, the signed result delivery, the scoring and the payout all run exactly as before. The untested part returns naturally with the first real supplier, who is on a different address by definition. And the question this raises has a reassuring answer: a real external supplier needs none of this. They host their own game on their own address and we simply store it — so no domain entry and no web-server change is ever needed to add one. This work was the one-time cost of being our own supplier, not a cost that repeats.

The two halves met on 7 September 2026, and the paragraphs above are correct as history rather than as a description of today. A round now travels the whole way between the platform and the game company: the platform creates it, a player's moves are made against a real board, the game company works out the score, sends it back signed over a real network connection, and the platform pays out real prize money on the strength of it. Everything above that says the two halves have never met, or that no round has travelled between them, was true when it was written and is not true now. The notable thing is that this found nothing. Every earlier step in this programme uncovered a genuine fault on first contact; the first real round uncovered none in the product. Two things are still stood in for and should not be glossed over: the web-address layer the platform normally receives the result through, and the browser itself. So this is still not the sign-off, which asks for a person to click through a whole round on a deployed system, and that remains the next thing to do.

The game itself was then rebuilt on the owner's report that the board was small and ugly, had no instructions, and finished with a poor result screen. All three were true, and all three were invisible to every test we had. They lived in the one file that cannot be examined automatically, because it only works inside a real browser window — so the work was as much about moving those decisions somewhere they can be checked as about the appearance itself. The board was not merely small: it was locked at the smallest size the code can draw, by a circular measurement. The game measured the window it had been given in order to ask for a bigger one, but its own layout sizes itself to that same window — so it measured, asked for exactly what it already had, and both sides agreed the game should stay in the small box it opened in. Every player, on every screen, got the smallest possible board. It now asks for the size the puzzle actually needs, and the squares roughly doubled. The result screen was worse than untidy: it told the winner they had run out of time. One of the two games ends when a player completes every board, and the other when a clock expires; both endings are recorded under the same word internally, so the screen greeted them identically. The player who had done everything the game asked was congratulated for running out of time. Every ending now has its own wording, and each one says what happens next — including that a round the system voids returns the attempt rather than costing it. And the instructions had been written down twice, once inside the game screen and once in the catalogue the platform reads, and the two had already drifted apart. They are now written once. The related gap was quieter and mattered more: the game never told the player how it was scored. In a paid competition, not knowing whether a fast board beats a completed one is not a presentational shortcoming — for one of the two games it is the difference between playing to win and playing to lose. Verified by eye, on both games, on a desktop and a phone — which is worth contrasting with the platform's own competition screens, which sit behind sign-in and could not be looked at at all.

Then, on 8 September 2026, the owner opened the game on the live server and it did not start at all — and the cause was not a fault in anything anybody had written. The game's screen is a set of ordinary files that arrive with a code update. The list authorising the service to hand those files to a browser is part of the program, and only changes when the program is rebuilt. The server had been updated but not rebuilt, so it was an older program serving a newer screen: one file the screen depends on was refused as unknown. The way that fails is the part worth understanding, because it explains why nothing detected it. A missing file of this kind does not produce a partly-working game — the browser abandons everything that depended on it, so no part of the game ran, and the player watched a loading spinner for ever. Nothing failed from the platform's side, nothing appeared in any log on either side, and the only component in the entire system that noticed anything was wrong was the delay warning added to the player's screen the day before.

The same stale program explains the two complaints that looked unrelated. The discontinued puzzle was still being offered when creating a competition, and the surviving one still advertised a five-minute limit that had been raised to an hour — both because the competition wizard reads a copy of the catalogue taken from that service, and no fresh copy had been taken. The screen that offers the games was already correct and needed no change, which was worth confirming before building anything: hiding discontinued games was already its behaviour, and there was simply nothing discontinued in the copy it had been given. No test could have caught this and none was written to try. The existing check that every file the screen needs is authorised passed all day, and correctly — it examines one version of the code, and this fault is a disagreement between two versions. Only the running server can answer that, so the service now checks its own two halves the moment it starts, names the offending file, and says to rebuild. It reports rather than refusing to start, deliberately: results for games already in progress are delivered by the same process, and refusing to start would turn a broken screen into competitions that cannot pay out. A second fault surfaced while reading the logs for the first: when the service failed to deliver a result to the platform it recorded only that a delivery had failed, while the reason — a wrong password, a platform that is down, an address pointing nowhere — had already been worked out and was being discarded. A player sat watching “confirming your result” while the one line in the log that could have explained it said nothing. Nothing was at risk financially, no money moved and no prize was affected; the cost was that players who never got a game had still used up their attempt. The remedy is a rebuild, a restart and a refresh of the catalogue.

That remedy was right and the safeguard shipped with it was worthless, which the owner demonstrated within the hour by opening the game again and watching it fail in exactly the same way. The service was to check its own two halves at startup and say which file it could not serve. It cannot: the check is part of the program, so in the one situation it exists for — a program too old to know about the file — the check is too old to be there at all. It was real, tested, deliberately broken to prove the tests caught it, and absent from the only server that needed it. The general lesson is worth more than the fault: a warning light installed inside the thing whose failure it reports is dark precisely when it matters, so the answer to two things that must be shipped together is to stop having two things. The list of files the service will serve is no longer written in the program at all — it is simply what is in the folder when the service starts. A new file works the moment it arrives, because there is no second copy of the truth left to disagree with the first. The safeguards that mattered are unchanged: a browser can still only ask for a file by name from a fixed set built at startup, so there is no way to walk out of that folder, and only recognised kinds of file are offered at all. The startup check is kept for the one job still left to it — naming a file nobody thought to make servable — and the honest state is that the server still needs one rebuild, because the fix itself is in the program.

The owner then opened it a third time and reported the same endless spinner — and the third report is the finding, not the fault. Both earlier fixes attacked the cause: first the list of files, then the arrangement that let the list go stale. Neither of them made the failure say anything. So the only account of what had gone wrong was a technical log inside the owner's own browser, which they had to find, copy and send — and because that was the only evidence, every conversation about it turned into a disagreement about which machine was out of date rather than about the game. Removing the cause we found is worth doing and cannot promise that nothing between a player's browser and the game — a caching layer, a proxy, a copy of the refusal remembered from before the fix — ever withholds a file again.

So the game now explains itself. A small piece of code sits in front of the rest, deliberately written in the older style that cannot be brought down by the failure it is watching for, and if the game has not begun to run after eight seconds it says so on screen, names the file that was refused, offers a retry, and — the part that actually matters — tells the platform to take its loading cover off. Without that last step the explanation is painted underneath an opaque cover, where nobody can read it and the button to leave the round is unreachable. A warning nobody can see is worse than none, because it looks fixed. Eight seconds is chosen to be inside the platform's own twelve-second timeout: whichever waits longer is the one that speaks, and if it were this one the whole thing would be decoration.

And the fault inside that fix was found by looking at it rather than by testing it. The first version named the wrong file — it reported the main script, which had loaded perfectly, because the browser attributes the failure to whatever began the chain rather than to the file actually missing. Naming the wrong file is worse than naming none, because it sends whoever investigates to code that is correct. It now reads the browser's own record of the failed request. Both directions were then checked by eye: with a file deliberately withheld it names that file, and with everything present but the round unavailable the game's own error message appears and survives past the eight seconds — proving the new watchdog stays quiet rather than talking over a real diagnosis.

Then the owner reported it a fourth time, and brought proof that the server was right: a direct request for the missing file, run on both machines, returned it successfully — while the game on screen named that very file as the one it could not load. Both statements were true, and the contradiction is the entire answer. The refusal was no longer on any server. It was inside the player's own browser, and it had four hours to run. Everything the platform serves passes through a caching layer, and that layer rewrites the instruction governing how long a browser may keep a copy — to four hours, on failures exactly as on successes, discarding the instruction the game service sends, which says never to keep a failure at all. So a file that was genuinely absent for a few minutes that morning was recorded as absent by every browser that asked, for the rest of the morning, and no repair could reach them, because a browser holding a fresh answer does not ask the question again. The server was correct within minutes. The game stayed broken for hours. Every check available to us reported success.

The reason this took four attempts to find is that the obvious check cannot see it. A request run on the server has no stale copy of anything, so it can only ever confirm the half that was already right — which is precisely what it kept doing, and why the conversation kept returning to which machine was out of date. It was found by asking the question from a browser instead, and reading what came back rather than only whether it worked.

The cure has to live in the page, because nowhere else has standing to act. We cannot make an intermediary respect an instruction it is replacing, and no amount of correct deployment reaches a browser that has stopped asking. So before the game gives up and shows its message, it now asks for the missing files once more in the one manner that both ignores the stored answer and replaces it — and if they arrive, it reloads itself and carries on. The player sees a few seconds of loading instead of a dead game, and nobody has to be told anything. It takes exactly one such attempt, remembered in a way that survives the reload, because the alternative is a round that flickers in front of a paying player for ever; and if the file really is gone, the attempt fails and the honest message appears as before.

One half of this is fixed and one half is a setting nobody has changed yet, and the difference matters. Successful files carry the same four-hour instruction, so an improvement to the game does not reach a player who opened it earlier that afternoon — and that half cannot be detected from inside the page, because a slightly old game that works looks exactly like a current one. A single rule in the caching layer's control panel, telling it to respect what the game service says, removes the whole class of fault. Until that is done, the honest position is that any change to the game is invisible to anyone who played in the last four hours.

The same report contained a second complaint — that leaving the game was also stuck — and that one was a genuine fault on the platform's own side. Leaving a game correctly takes the player to a waiting screen rather than back to the competition, because the attempt has already been used and returning them silently would let them believe otherwise. But that screen waited a full minute before offering any way onward, and during that minute it showed a spinner and no button of any kind — to a player who had just pressed the one control on the screen meaning “get me out of here”. The reason this was missed is worth recording, because it looked like solved ground: two earlier pieces of work had removed waits that went on for ever, and this one did end, and did end somewhere truthful. A wait that ends is not the same thing as a wait you can leave. Sixty seconds with nothing to press is indistinguishable from the site having hung, and the player had already reached that conclusion before they pressed the button. The second half of it was simply untrue, and that was the worse fault: the screen said we were waiting for the game to confirm their score, when the reason people press that button is that the game never started — so there was no score and never had been. The screen now says two different true things depending on how the player got there, and both state plainly that they are free to leave, because the result is delivered to our own records by the game company and nothing about it depends on the player watching.

The same report raised a design question rather than a fault, and it is the most far-reaching thing in it. Every competition the platform can currently create assumes players turn up whenever they like: a puzzle sits there, and whoever solves it best wins. A race does not work that way — everybody has to start together. The owner's instinct was that a game should declare which sort it is, and the setup form should follow. That was right, and the field that looks like it already does this turns out to be about something else entirely. Each game already declares whether it needs an opponent, and the natural reading is that this also tells us whether players compete at the same moment. It does not: a race needs no opponent at all, because every runner runs their own track against the clock. What a race actually needs is a shared moment, and nothing in the specification describes one. Worth noting on its own: that field is required of every provider, is checked when a catalogue arrives, is shown on screen — and no decision anywhere is made from it. Its description claimed otherwise and has been corrected.

The reassuring half is that a simultaneous competition is mostly a configuration of machinery that already exists. Measured against the code rather than guessed at, seven things change when everybody must play at once, and five of them can already be expressed today: one attempt only, a playing window as long as one run, a refusal to start before the window opens, and the handling of somebody who never turns up, which was settled earlier this month. What is genuinely missing is the starting gun itself, and — more importantly — anything at all preventing an operator from setting a race up as though it were a puzzle. The recommendation is therefore to record the design and build it when a real race is signed, rather than issue a new version of the provider document describing something no game yet uses. One distinction in it must not be lost, because it is easy to overstate the problem: a race played at staggered times is not unfair. Everybody gets the same track and the same clock, so the times are genuinely comparable and the prizes are correct. What is lost is the occasion — the drama, the shared moment, people watching. That is a real requirement, but it is a product one, not a money one, and saying so keeps the register believable.

Mapping that question uncovered a live gap in the fairness rules, and it is now closed. The provider document calls one field the most important in the entire specification: the promise that every player in a competition faces identical content. Without it, two players are ranked against each other having attempted different puzzles of unknown relative difficulty — and it is also the foundation of the argument that these games are decided by skill rather than luck, which is what keeps them outside gambling regulation. Nothing in the platform was checking it. The field was demanded of providers, validated when a catalogue arrived, stored, passed around and displayed — and no part of the system ever consulted it before taking an entry fee. Three separate comments in three different files stated plainly that it did. That is the fifth time a note in the code has asserted a safeguard that was never performed, and the first time the claim appeared in three places at once, which is its own lesson: three comments agreeing is not confirmation, because the second and third were written by reading the first. What hid it is that the two neighbouring checks of the same kind are properly enforced, so anyone reading that area came away reassured. No unfair competition has run — every game that exists makes the promise — but it is exactly the promise a real provider will decline on some titles, and the failure would have been a paid competition where everyone played something different, ranked, settled and paid out in silence. The setup form now refuses such a title and says which promise is missing, rather than merely greying it out.
