The question: can ChartVolt really run games other than trading?
Yes — because the hard part is already built. Our competition and payment engine has never cared what the players were actually doing.
ChartVolt already contains a complete, working machine for “take an entry fee, run a contest, rank the players, pay the winners, refund if it is cancelled.” That machine works the same way whether the players are trading currencies or answering questions. Only four narrow parts of the product assume the activity is trading, and those are what this plan changes. Nothing has to be torn down and rebuilt.

1

Where we are going

One account, one wallet, one league table — and as many games as we want behind it.

In plain words

Today ChartVolt is one game with one audience: people who want to compete at trading. That is a real market, but it is a narrow one. After this project, ChartVolt is a place where people pay a small entry fee to compete at something they are good at — and trading is simply the first of those things. Same account, same wallet, same prizes, same trust.

What this unlocks

A much wider audience

Trading competitions only appeal to people already interested in markets. A quiz competition is understandable to anyone in five seconds. The entry fee, the prize and the leaderboard are identical — only the barrier to understanding drops.

A cheaper second and third game

The expensive part is the shared machinery, and it is already paid for. Once the design is in place, adding another game is mostly content and rules rather than engineering.

Clear numbers per game

For the first time we will be able to see entry-fee volume, revenue, average prize pot, how full contests get and payout ratio broken down by game — so we can tell which games earn and which merely attract.

Less dependence on one market

Today a quiet period in the currency markets is a quiet period for the whole business. Games that do not depend on market conditions or trading hours smooth that out.

The end state, stated plainly

ChartVolt becomes a competition platform where trading is one game among several. A visitor arrives at a catalogue of games, picks one, reads what it is, and enters a competition or challenges someone directly. Their wallet, level, badges, ranking and history follow them across every game they play. Trading is not reduced in any way — it keeps every feature it has today and remains the most sophisticated thing on the platform. It simply stops being the only door in, and stops being the thing the whole product is named after.

One consequence worth stating early, because it shapes the plan: the games catalogue is the last thing built, not the first. A catalogue containing one game other than trading advertises an empty platform, so the machinery and a real second game come first, and the shop window goes up once there is something to put in it. Section 15 sets out the order.

What we already own

The reason this is a 14–19 week project rather than a rebuild.

In plain words

ChartVolt already runs the full lifecycle of a paid contest: collect the entry fee → run the event → rank the players → pay the winners → refund everyone if it is cancelled. None of that machinery asks what the players were doing. That is the single most important fact in this document.

The contest lifecycleDraft, upcoming, live, finishing, completed, cancelled. Works for a quiz exactly as it does for trading.

The conclusion

The competition engine and the money engine are sound and proven with real money. This project adds a dimension to them rather than replacing them. Nothing that currently works has to be removed.

3

What we reuse, and what we build

Four clearly-defined areas. That is the whole engineering project.

Deliberately not on the list

We are not deleting trading, not rewriting the payment system, not splitting the wallet, and not running a second website. Each of those was considered and rejected as more expensive and far riskier than the approach above.

4

How it will work

One shared engine, many plug-in games.

In plain words

Every contest gets a label saying which game it is. Each game then has its own small plug-in that knows three things: how to set the game up, how to score it, and how to finish it. The shared engine stops caring what the game is — it only knows that players earn a score, scores produce finishing positions, and positions receive prizes.

What each game brings, and what stays shared

The game provides

  • Its own settings, and a check that an operator’s choices make sense
  • What a player starts with
  • How players are scored and ranked
  • How the contest is finished and final scores produced
  • Its own screens and its own language

The platform provides

  • Entry fees, wallet and full audit trail
  • The prize pot and how it splits by position
  • Our fee and unclaimed prize handling
  • Refunds and cancellations
  • Fraud and eligibility checks
  • Levels, badges, leaderboards, notifications

Two things kept separate

It is worth understanding the difference, because it explains the grid of products we can offer:

IdeaOptionsWhat it decides
Contest formatCompetition (two or more players) or challenge (exactly two)How many players and how the prizes split
GameTrading, trivia, and more laterWhat the players actually do

Today we have two formats and one game. The target is two formats and many games — so a single new game immediately gives us more than one product, and a game can decline a format that does not suit it.

5

Points and leaderboards

The most interesting design problem: making different games fairly comparable.

In plain words — the problem

A trader might finish with a profit of +4,382. A quiz player might finish with a score of 87. You cannot put those in one league table — the trader would always look better, which means nothing. So we need a fair way to convert both into the same currency.

Points versus rating

Points reward playing a lot

They accumulate over a season, drive progression and can decide seasonal prizes. A player who competes often will climb.

Rating rewards playing well

A per-game skill number, shown as something like “Trading 1840 · Trivia 1610”. It rises and falls with results rather than volume, so it cannot be farmed by simply entering everything.

Showing both tells an honest story and gives players two different reasons to come back. ChartVolt has no rating system today, and it is the single feature that will make several games feel like one product rather than a collection.

The league tables players will see

TableAnswers the question
Per game“Who are the best quiz players?” “Who are the best traders?”
Overall“Who are the best players on ChartVolt?”
Per contest“Who is winning this event right now?”
A performance win along the way

The global league table is currently worked out from scratch every time someone opens it, which makes it slow and limits how many players it can show. As part of this work we keep a running total per player instead, so the table loads almost instantly and can show everyone rather than a capped list. Players will feel this immediately.

Fair play by design

Every new game invites new ways to cheat, so the counter-measures are designed in from the start rather than added after an incident:

The loopholeHow it is closed
Farming points in tiny private contestsPoints scale with the size of the field, and a minimum number of players is required before points count at all
Two friends trading wins back and forthRepeated challenges against the same opponent earn steeply reduced points, and our existing collusion detection extends to all games
One person using several accountsExisting identity, device and location checks, plus entry limits that count every game rather than trading alone
Sharing quiz answers between playersEveryone sees the same questions but in a different order, so telling a friend “the answer is B” is useless
Faking a fast answerAll timing is measured on our servers and never taken from the player’s device
6

Money and prizes

The best news in the plan: this barely changes at all.

In plain words

A quiz contest handles money in exactly the same way a trading contest does. A player pays 10 credits to enter, the pot becomes 10 credits times the number of players, ChartVolt takes its percentage, the top positions receive their shares, and anything left over is redistributed or recorded as unclaimed. No new payment work, no new provider, no new risk.

Two details specific to quizzes

Ties will be common

On a ten-question quiz many players will score the same. Splitting shared positions equally becomes the sensible default, and operators are warned before choosing winner-takes-all for a game where ties are likely.

A minimum-effort rule

Trading contests already require a minimum number of trades so nobody can enter and do nothing. The quiz equivalent is a minimum number of questions answered before a player qualifies for prizes.

What we will be able to report

Once every contest carries a game label, the financial dashboard can break down entry-fee volume, revenue, average pot size, how full contests get and payout ratio — by game. That answers the question we cannot currently answer: is a given game genuinely profitable, or is it a cheap way of acquiring customers who then do something else?

Regulatory position

The money structure stays deliberately identical across every game: a fixed entry fee, a pooled prize, and a fixed prize based on finishing position. Adding more skill games with that same structure strengthens our position, because it shows the business monetises competitive skill in general rather than exposure to financial markets. Each new game is assessed on its own merits for skill before launch, and prizes will never be tied to a continuous measure of performance.

A second revenue line, and a limit we are placing on it deliberately

The platform already sells items — indicators and bots for trading. As the catalogue grows, that store can carry items for every game, with ownership tracked per game. It is worth doing, but it earns very little until several games have a real population playing them, so it is not on the critical path.

Nothing sold will improve a player’s score or ranking in a paid contest

This rule gives up some revenue, and we are giving it up on purpose, for three reasons that each stand on their own. Trust: in a contest with real prize money, a player who discovers that a paying rival had an advantage does not complain — they leave, and they tell others. Regulation: our entire position rests on outcomes being decided by skill; the moment spending measurably changes results, that argument weakens. Payments: “I paid for an advantage and still lost” produces card disputes, and dispute rates are what put a merchant account at risk. The cost of this rule is a modest amount of forgone revenue. The cost of breaking it is the business.

What we will sell

  • Appearance — avatars, frames, name colours, themes
  • Titles and display badges, cosmetic only
  • Entry-fee vouchers and discounts
  • Extra practice rounds, outside ranked play
  • Richer personal statistics and history

What we will not sell

  • Anything that adds points or score
  • Extra time, lives, retries, hints or skips
  • Extra attempts in a one-attempt contest
  • Easier content inside a ranked contest
  • Random or mystery packs, even cosmetic ones

The last one deserves a word of explanation, because loot boxes are a large and proven revenue line elsewhere. Paying real money for a randomised reward introduces exactly the element of chance that our regulatory position depends on being absent, and it makes no difference that the item is only decorative. Where packs are commercially attractive, we will sell known contents at a known price.

Levels, badges and the player journey

Mostly a content exercise rather than an engineering one.

In plain words

The systems that award experience, hand out badges and guide a player’s journey are already built and already editable from the admin panel. Most of the work here is writing new content — new badge names, new milestones — not new software.

SystemCurrent stateWhat changes

A free win on day one

Many existing badges are already game-neutral in substance — contests entered, wins, podium finishes, deposits, account verification, social badges. Simply marking those as applying to all games gives quiz players a full, meaningful badge collection from launch at no content cost at all.

The rule that protects the experience

A player who has never traded will never be shown trading badges or trading achievements. A list of goals you can never complete is worse than no list — it actively puts people off. Every player sees only what is achievable for the games they actually play.

8

Running the platform

What changes for the team who create and manage contests.

In plain words

Creating a contest today is a step-by-step wizard. We add one new step at the start — “which game?” — and two of the later steps then adapt to the game chosen. Everything else an operator already knows stays the same.

A clearer menu

Today competitions live under a menu heading called “Trading”. Once quizzes exist, that no longer makes sense. The admin panel reorganises into three clear groups:

COMPETITIONS

Every contest, whatever the game, with a filter by game. Plus a new screen for switching games on and off.

TRADING

Trading-only tools: history, market hours, instruments, market data, risk settings. Hidden entirely if trading is switched off.

TRIVIA

Question bank, categories and quiz settings. Appears only when trivia is switched on.

Creating a contest

StepWhat changes
1. Which gameNew Choose the game. Locked once players have joined.
2–4. Basics, entry fee, scheduleUnchanged
5. Game settingsAdapts Trading shows today’s screen exactly. Trivia shows category, number of questions, seconds per question and difficulty mix.
6. PrizesUnchanged
7. RulesAdapts The ranking options offered come from the chosen game.
8. Review and launchUnchanged, with the game shown prominently

The question bank

This is the largest new admin screen, and it is what determines whether the quiz game feels fair and fresh:

  • Bulk import, so thousands of questions can be loaded rather than typed one at a time.
  • Duplicate detection, so the same question never appears twice in one contest.
  • Statistics per question. A question nearly everyone gets right tells us nothing about skill and is retired; one nearly everyone gets wrong is probably mistaken. This is the quality loop that keeps the game honest.
  • AI-assisted drafting with human approval. The platform already generates other content with AI, so drafting questions is natural reuse — but every question is approved by a person before it can appear in a paid contest.
An operating rule worth knowing

We keep at least ten times as many questions as any single contest uses. That is what stops regular players seeing the same questions repeatedly, and it is a content commitment rather than a software one.

What players see

One familiar journey, with only the middle step differing by game.

How players find a game

This is the change with the biggest commercial consequence, so it is worth being precise about it. Today a player arrives at a list of competitions. In the new design a player arrives at a catalogue of games — each with its own page, its own artwork, its own description and its own league table — and picks the one that appeals to them. The competitions for that game are shown on the game's own page.

Why the catalogue matters commercially

Three things it buys us. A reason to return — a catalogue that grows is a reason to check back, in a way a longer list of contests is not. Search traffic — a page per game is a page that can be found by people searching for that game, rather than for trading. A place to promote — featuring a game, launching one, or announcing one as coming soon becomes a merchandising decision made in the admin panel in minutes, not a development task.

Each game's page is edited like a product listing

Name, tagline, description, rules, how to play, artwork, category and position in the catalogue are all editable in the admin panel while the site is running. Adding a seasonal variant, refreshing artwork or promoting a game to the front row needs no developer and no release. That is deliberate: merchandising should move at marketing speed, not release speed.

The personal dashboard

The dashboard currently shows six trading figures — capital, profit, open positions, trades, win rate and so on. A quiz-only player would see six empty boxes.

The rule we follow

A player sees one section for each game they actually play, and nothing for games they do not. No empty boxes, no jargon they did not ask for. A shared header carries the things that matter to everyone: credits, total points, rating and active contests.

Help and guidance

Our help centre is substantial and good. Rather than rewriting it, we split it into a shared core — account, wallet, deposits, withdrawals, verification, levels, badges, fair play, support — plus one guide per game. The existing trading content is kept word for word as the trading guide, and a new quiz guide is written alongside it.

The platform speaks about competitions; each game keeps its own voice.

In plain words

Today the product calls people “traders” and calls its section “Trading”. That has to become neutral in the shared parts of the product, while each game keeps its own natural language inside its own screens. A trader still sees trading words; a quiz player sees quiz words; the shell around both is game-neutral.

Two things worth knowing

1. The wording lives in the admin panel, so it can be adjusted at any time without a software release. Language attracts opinions, and this means opinions can be acted on the same day.

2. The measure of success is that a trader should not notice this project happened. The trading terminal, its charts, its help guide and its alerts all keep their existing language deliberately.

The Terms of Service and risk disclosures currently describe a trading competition platform. Those are contractual documents tied to our regulatory position, so they are handled on a legal track running in parallel with the build — not treated as copywriting.

11

The Trivia game

The first new game, chosen deliberately.

In plain words

A timed quiz competition. Players pay to enter, answer a set of multiple-choice questions, and are ranked by score. The top positions share the pot using exactly the same prize structure trading competitions use today. Scoring rewards being right, being fast, and being consistent.

Why trivia first

Instantly understandable

No financial knowledge required. A new visitor understands the offer within seconds, which is the opposite of a trading competition.

No new infrastructure

It needs no market data, no live price feed and no new service — just questions and screens. That keeps the first new game cheap and low-risk.

Defensible as skill

Knowledge and recall speed are clearly skill rather than chance, which keeps our regulatory position consistent with trading.

It proves the design

Trivia is about as different from trading as a game can be. If the shared engine handles both cleanly, it will handle almost anything we add later.

How scoring works

For each question:

  • Wrong, or out of time → no points
  • Correct → base points, plus a speed bonus for answering sooner, plus a streak bonus for consecutive correct answers

The total decides the ranking, and total time taken breaks ties — which matters, because ties on a short quiz are common.

Keeping it fair

The concernHow it is handled
“People will look up the answers”A tight timer per question, and a speed bonus that rewards instant recall over searching
“People will share answers”Everyone gets the same questions in a different order, so naming an option is useless
“Automated players”Device checks, a minimum believable answer time, and review of impossible speed-and-accuracy combinations
“The same questions keep appearing”Questions rotate by how often they have been used, backed by the ten-to-one content rule
“My connection dropped mid-question”Reloading returns the same question with the correct remaining time, so nobody loses a paid contest to a dropped signal
“Could a result be disputed?”The question set is fixed when the contest starts, so any result can be recalculated exactly and shown to be correct
Deliberately kept for later

The first version lets players take the quiz at their own pace within the contest window. A live version — everyone answering the same question at the same moment, with a visible score race — is a much better spectacle and a clear future step, but it is significantly harder to build. We ship the simpler version first, learn from real players, then invest in the show.

12

Trading becomes optional

A business capability, not a technical detail.

In plain words

We gain a single setting that turns trading on or off across the whole product. With it off, the platform still works completely — it simply offers other games. This matters for entering markets where trading competitions are awkward, for launching a games-only brand, and for reducing our dependence on one activity.

Market flexibility

Some territories are simpler to enter without a trading product. The same platform can serve them with games only.

White-label options

A partner brand can be launched as games-only, or trading-only, from the same platform.

Nothing is thrown away

Trading is switched off, never deleted. It can be switched back on at any time with no rebuild.

The safeguard that comes with it

The switch cannot be used to strand players. If trading contests are still running, the system will decline to switch trading off and instead offer to switch it off once those contests finish. Open positions are always able to be priced and closed properly.

How we keep it safe

The safeguards that protect trading, protect the money, and protect us from surprises.

In plain words

The two things that must never go wrong are: trading customers noticing a change, and prize money going to the wrong person. Everything below exists to make both impossible rather than merely unlikely.

Protecting the trading business

SafeguardWhat it does
Proof, not opinionWe recalculate a batch of past, completed competitions through the new system and confirm the resulting leaderboards are identical to the ones actually recorded at the time. That turns “we think trading still works” into evidence.
Move, do not rewriteThe trading game is created by relocating the existing proven code rather than rewriting it, which is what makes the proof above possible.
Trading keeps its own languageThe terminal, its help guide and its alerts are deliberately excluded from the language changes.
Nothing removedTrading infrastructure is kept permanently and simply left idle when not in use.

Protecting the money

SafeguardWhat it does
Two independent checks before paying outContests are checked for which game they are before any settlement work begins, and the trading settlement itself refuses to run on a non-trading contest. Two barriers on the one path where an error would cost real money.
Every payout is reconciledPrizes paid plus our fee must equal the prize pot for every contest, with an alert if they ever differ.
Paying twice is impossibleFinishing a contest can be safely repeated without paying anyone a second time.
Real-money rehearsalBefore any public launch we run internal quiz contests with genuine, very small entry fees, so the payment path is proven with real money rather than assumed.
Nothing is destroyedEvery change to our records adds information rather than altering or removing it, so every step can be reversed cleanly.

The risks we are actively managing

RiskSeverityHow it is managed
A quiz contest being settled as if it were a trading contestCriticalTwo independent checks, plus monitoring of every settlement by game
Fair-play limits not counting the new gameHighEntry and fraud limits are updated to count all games together
Quiz scoring being gamedHighServer-side timing, shuffled options, minimum answer times, anomaly review
Legal wording changed without reviewHighTerms and disclosures handled on a separate legal track from the start
Players unhappy about leaderboard changesMediumThe new table is built and compared alongside the old one before switching, and the change is explained clearly in advance
Not enough questions at launchMediumAvailability is confirmed when a contest is created, and the ten-to-one content rule is an operating commitment
Scope creep into further games too earlyMediumThe design is built for exactly two games. The third comes only after trivia is live and learned from
On scope discipline

Our strategy work names several possible games — chess, esports, fantasy sports, coding contests. The temptation is to design for all of them now. We will not. Two games is the minimum number that forces a genuinely reusable design, and more than two before either is live means designing against guesses. Ship trivia, learn from real players, then add the third.

How we launch

Nothing is visible to players until we choose, and every step can be reversed.

#StepPlayers see it?
1Groundwork put in place behind the scenes. Unused for now.No
2The shared engine learns about games, and trading becomes the first game. The identical-results proof must pass.No
3Trivia built, admin screens built — still switched off.No
4Question bank filled. Trivia enabled for internal accounts only.No
5Internal quiz contests with real, very small entry fees. Money proven end to end.No
6Trivia opened to everyone, starting free or very low cost.Yes
7New leaderboard built and checked against the existing one before any switch.No
8Leaderboard switched over; new points and rating explained to players in advance.Yes
9Language and interface updates.Yes
10Trading on/off switch delivered, still switched on. Rehearsed before ever being used.No
Why step 5 is non-negotiable

A paid contest that has never been run with real money is an untested payment path. Small internal contests with genuine fees are the cheapest possible way to discover a problem — before a customer does.

What “finished” means

An operator can create, run and complete a quiz competition without touching a single trading setting.
A player pays credits, plays, is ranked and is paid through the same money system as trading.
A trading customer’s experience is demonstrably unchanged — proven by recalculating past contests, not by opinion.
With trading switched off, the platform is complete and coherent: no dead links, no empty panels, no stranded contests.
Leaderboards, points, rating, levels, badges and the journey all work for a quiz-only player.
We can report revenue and payout ratio by game.
Adding a third game needs rules and content only — no change to the shared engine.

That final point is the real test of whether we built a platform or just another feature.

Why the most visible part is built last

The instinct is to build the shop window first. It is the wrong instinct here, for one blunt reason: a games catalogue containing one game other than trading advertises an empty platform. The catalogue is also the cheapest and lowest-risk piece to add, while the expensive and delicate work sits underneath it. So the order is: build the machinery, get a real second game running and being played, then build the window and fill it.

A natural decision point at week 8

Phases 1 to 3 end with a quiz that can be created, played and paid for. That is a real product, and it is a sensible place to stop and look at the evidence before funding the rest: are players entering, are they coming back, does the second game hold attention the way trading does? Committing the whole programme up front is not necessary and not recommended. The main risk to this plan is scope, not technical difficulty.

What we would ask partners to weigh in on
